Welcome

About this toolbox

This book collects workshops that youth workers designed, ran and improved together during "Green Facilitator", an Erasmus+ training course held from 25 October to 2 November 2026 in the forests of Sarnena Gora near Stara Zagora, Bulgaria.

Participants from Poland, Bulgaria, Romania, Turkey, Spain and Hungary learned to run environmental workshops that really reach young people: activities that use nature, the senses and play instead of lectures, and that make room for every voice in the group. Each team then designed its own workshop, tested it with the group, listened to honest feedback and wrote down what they learned.

Everything here is ready to use with young people. Take it, adapt it to your group, and make it your own.

How each workshop is laid out

  • At a glance: time, group size, space and materials, and who it is for.
  • What it is and why: a short summary, the aims and what participants take away.
  • Step by step: instructions you can follow on the day.
  • Tips and lessons from the test run: what the authors learned when they ran it, so you can skip their mistakes.
  • Debrief questions: to turn the experience into learning. These were added by the editors.

Before you start

The Green Facilitator mindset

The workshops in this book grew out of a few simple ideas the group practised all week. Keep them in mind and any activity will land better.

Experience first, theory second

Let people feel nature before you talk about it. A blind walk or an hour of colour-hunting builds a stronger connection than any presentation.

Hope over fear

Many young people feel eco-anxiety or simply switch off. Don't lecture about catastrophes; meet the feeling and turn it into something people can actually do.

Every voice counts

Use formats where quiet people get time to think and the loudest don't decide everything, like 1-2-4-All and 25/10 Crowd Sourcing (see the bonus methods).

Creativity makes it stick

Turning collected waste into junk art, or photos into a story, makes environmental topics personal, fun and memorable.

Test, listen, improve

Every workshop here was run once, then changed after honest feedback. Plan your first run as a test, and expect to adjust.

Safe and inclusive outdoors

Check the weather and the ground, agree clear boundaries, give everyone a way to opt out, and ask before taking or sharing photos.

Color-hunting Photoshoot in Nature

Developed by Dila Yıldırım (Turkey), İsmail Emre Şendur (Turkey) and Mateusz Kurzyk (Poland)

Time
1 hour
Group
16 people, in groups of 4
Space
Outdoors in nature, plus a screen
For
Anyone who enjoys being in nature

What it is

Small groups each get a colour and an emotion. They go out into nature, photograph everything they find in their colour, and connect the photos into a story about their emotion. Then each group tells its story to everyone.

Aims

To bring the group together around a common purpose and a shared emotion, raising awareness of nature and helping people get to know each other better.

Participants take away

  • A sharper eye for the details and beauty of nature
  • Storytelling skills
  • Closer bonds within their small group

Materials

Phones or cameras (at least one per group) and a projector or screen to show the photos.

Step by step

  1. Form groups 5 minSplit everyone into groups of four. Give each group one colour and one emotion.
  2. Hunt and shoot 30 minGroups head into nature and photograph things in their colour. Within the same time, they choose their best photos and link them into a story that expresses their emotion.
  3. Tell the story 25 minBack together, each group shows its photos on the screen and tells its story to the whole group.

Tips from the authors

  • Pick colours that are easy to find in nature, like green, brown, yellow or blue.
  • Choose emotions that suit your target group, and change them freely.
  • Give very clear instructions for the storytelling part, for example how many photos and how many minutes each group gets.

Debrief questions

  • What did you notice in nature that you would normally walk past?
  • How did your colour change the way you looked around?
  • Which story moved you most, and why?

Blind Tourist Guide

Developed by Yasmina Simofi (Romania), Nedko Zhelev (Bulgaria) and Bartłomiej Bzowski (Poland)

Time
30–40 minutes
Group
Small groups of 3–4
Space
Outdoors, near objects or buildings
For
People ready to step out of their comfort zone

What it is

One person is blindfolded and becomes a "tour guide". An assistant walks them to an object or building, and the "tourists" ask questions about it. The guide has to improvise a convincing tourist explanation without seeing anything.

Themes

How easily tour guides, and anyone who sounds confident, can spread misinformation. Also observation, trust and improvisation.

Aims

To bring awareness to the surroundings and to how we sense the place we are in.

Participants take away

  • Confidence in improvising
  • A new awareness of their surroundings

Materials

A blindfold for each group.

Debrief questions

  • How did it feel to explain something you couldn't see?
  • Did the tourists believe the guide? Why does confident-sounding information feel true?
  • Where else do we meet information that sounds right but isn't?

Step by step

  1. Set the rolesIn each small group, choose a tour guide, an assistant and one or two tourists.
  2. Blindfold and walkThe assistant blindfolds the tour guide and carefully leads them to an object or building.
  3. Tourists askThe tourists ask questions about it, such as "What is this used for?" or "What is the history behind it?"
  4. Guide improvisesThe tour guide makes up a touristic explanation. They win if they manage to work out which object or building it really is.
  5. Swap rolesRotate so that everyone gets a turn as the guide.

Lesson from the test run

We played in one big group and too many people were passive. Keep the groups small, three or four people, so everyone is involved all the time.

Safety tip

Assistants are responsible for their guide: walk slowly, warn about steps and obstacles, and never let go.

Dodgeball in Nature

Developed by Ege Muslu (Turkey), Çağrı Çiçek (Turkey), Elmira Ketenci (Turkey) and Réka Szalay (Hungary)

Time
45–60 minutes
Group
16–20 people, two teams
Space
Flat area, at least 20 × 10 m
For
Young people getting to know each other

What it is

A classic team game moved outdoors. Two teams play dodgeball in nature, with a few extra rules that reward catching the ball and give eliminated players a way back in.

Aims

To promote team building and cooperation while being in contact with nature, through a fun physical activity that gets everyone moving.

Participants take away

  • Bonding through team sport
  • A conversation about cultural differences: every country plays dodgeball a little differently

Materials

A soft ball, comfortable sports clothes and, ideally, sunny warm weather.

Lessons from the test run

  • Choosing the teams took so long that people lost their motivation. Pick teams fast and make them equal, for example by counting off.
  • The rules weren't clear before the game started, so it became controversial. Explain every rule, and check everyone understood, before the first throw.

How to play

  1. Set upSplit into two equal teams. The shooting team stands at both ends of the field; the evading team stands in the middle.
  2. Shoot and dodgeShooters throw the ball to hit the players in the middle. Middle players dodge, or try to catch the ball before it touches the ground.
  3. Extra livesA player who catches the ball earns an extra life. They can keep it for themselves or use it to bring an eliminated teammate back into the game.
  4. Win the roundThe evading team wins the round if they catch the ball in the air three times, or if their last remaining player survives 10 throws without being hit.
  5. Best of fiveSwitch roles between rounds. The first team to win three rounds wins the game.

Debrief questions

  • How did your team decide what to do? Who took the lead?
  • How is dodgeball played where you come from? What surprised you?
  • How did it feel to bring a teammate back into the game?

Walk of Shame

Developed by Sude Sertdurak (Türkiye), Iman Bihich (Spain) and Octavian Osvar (Romania)

Time
30 minutes
Group
10–30, including facilitators
Space
A large room or open outdoor area
For
Youth, young adults and youth workers

What it is

A two-part non-formal education session. First, a "Step Forward" (privilege walk): everyone takes on a character and steps forward each time a statement is true for that character. Then a "Walk and Talk": small groups stroll together, discussing reflective topics to build real connections.

Themes

Human rights, socio-economic inequality, social inclusion, empathy and interpersonal communication.

Aims

  • Raise awareness of systemic inequalities and the unequal distribution of privilege.
  • Foster empathy by stepping into the shoes of people from very different backgrounds.
  • Encourage active listening, self-reflection and bonding through guided conversations.

Participants take away

  • Knowledge: social stratification, human rights and systemic barriers.
  • Skills: active listening and empathetic communication.
  • Attitudes: tolerance, openness and stronger group cohesion.

Materials

Printed character cards (one per participant), the list of statements for the facilitator, and a timer. All are on the next two pages.

Step by step

  1. Hand out rolesGive out the character cards at random. Participants read them silently, get into character and imagine their daily life.
  2. Step forwardLine everyone up side by side. Read the statements one by one; participants take one step forward whenever a statement is true for their character.
  3. Debrief in a circleAsk people at the front and at the back how they felt. Talk about the realities of human rights and inequality.
  4. Walk and talkForm groups of 3–4 and give each a topic. Groups walk around the venue or outdoors while they talk. Every 10 minutes, rotate the groups and give a new topic.
  5. Final reflectionBring everyone back together to share key takeaways from their conversations.

Tips from the authors

  • Keep strict silence during the Step Forward exercise; it builds focus and impact.
  • The debrief can trigger strong emotions. Create a safe, non-judgmental space.
  • During Walk and Talk, encourage people to team up with someone they haven't spoken to yet.
  • Thirty minutes is tight for both parts. Allow extra time for a deeper debrief with larger groups.

Statements for the facilitator

Read slowly, one at a time. Participants step forward if the statement is true for their character.

  1. I have continuous access to clean, safe drinking water inside my home.
  2. I can walk alone at night in my neighbourhood without fear for my physical safety or of harassment.
  3. If I get sick or injured today, I can access quality medical care without falling into severe debt.
  4. I live in a home with secure heating, electricity and protection from extreme weather.
  5. I do not have to worry about where my next meal will come from.
  6. I have legal identification documents that let me freely access basic public services where I live.
  7. I have never felt threatened or discriminated against by police or security forces because of my background or status.
  8. I hold a passport or document that lets me cross international borders easily, without severe visa restrictions.
  9. I can publicly express my political opinions or identity without fear of persecution, violence or losing my job.
  10. I am confident I will not face employment discrimination based on my ethnicity, nationality, gender or social background.
  11. I feel fully protected by the legal system in the country where I live.
  12. I can access the internet, news and information freely, without state censorship.
  13. I could cover an unexpected emergency expense, such as a major repair or a hospital bill, without borrowing money.
  14. Most of my daily food comes from eating out at restaurants or ordering delivery.
  15. I own property or will inherit valuable assets from my family.
  16. I can afford higher education without long-term debt or working several full-time jobs.
  17. I can afford to travel abroad for holidays at least three times a year.
  18. I can see private healthcare specialists whenever I want to skip public waiting lists.
  19. If I make a mistake or break a minor law, my family's resources or status can protect me from serious consequences.
  20. I have never been forced to work in unsafe, illegal or abusive conditions to survive.
  21. I am confident my children or the next generation will enjoy a higher standard of living than I do.
  22. I can take long unpaid breaks or a career sabbatical whenever I feel overworked.

Topic ideas for Walk and Talk

  • Favourite season
  • What you value in a friend
  • The kind of travel you like: hiking, cities, food…
  • Your love language, and how you show appreciation
  • Something you'd like to improve about yourself: self-esteem, communication, discipline, confidence or emotional regulation

Bonus

Methods from the course

The trainers used these activities with the group during the week. They work with almost any topic, and especially well with ecology and climate action.

Blind walk

Pairs · outdoors · 30–45 min

In pairs, one person is blindfolded and the other guides. Explore nature through sound, touch and smell, everything except sight, then swap. It shows how much we miss when we only look, and builds a direct connection to nature.

Clean-up and junk art

Whole group · outdoors · half a day

Clean up a forest, a park or a street, then turn what you collected into art and sculptures. At Green Facilitator, old tyres, plastic bottles, a broken lamp and a lot of duct tape became "Contemporary art be like: sometimes junk is just junk". Every piece of waste ends up somewhere.

1-2-4-All

Liberating Structure · 12–15 min

  1. 1 min: think alone, in silence.
  2. 2 min: share in pairs.
  3. 4 min: in fours, look for similarities and differences.
  4. 5 min: each four shares its key idea with everyone.

Try: "If you were 10 times bolder, what environmental action would you do with young people this year? What's the first step?" Starting in silence gives introverts and less confident English speakers time to prepare.

25/10 Crowd Sourcing

Liberating Structure · 30 min

Everyone writes one bold idea and a first step on a card, for example: "What would make our next Erasmus+ project almost zero-waste?" People walk around swapping cards until "stop", then pairs rate the cards they hold from 1 to 5. After five rounds, count down from 25 and read out the top 10. Ratings are anonymous, so there is no pressure or competition.

TRIZ: how do we destroy the planet?

Liberating Structure · 35–40 min

In groups of 4–5, list how to organise "the worst and least sustainable youth exchange about ecology ever": only talk about catastrophes, shame people, lecture instead of act, use difficult words, never ask young people's opinion. Then ask honestly: "What on this list do we already do, even a little?" Decide what to stop and the first step. Fun, unblocking, and never preachy.

Quiz games with Blooket

Teams · indoors · 20–30 min per quiz

Teams build an interactive quiz on blooket.com, for example "What is fast fashion?", and run it live for the rest of the group. A free, ready-to-use digital tool that turns any topic into a game.
